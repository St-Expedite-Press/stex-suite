# Sandbatch's Notes

*Not only the arrangement, but a good deal of the incidental method of this book, was suggested by the practice of appending to a poem the very apparatus that pretends to explain it — a practice I did not invent and will not apologize for borrowing.* Eliot did it first and best, prefacing his own notes to *The Waste Land* with the disclaimer that "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem were suggested by Miss Jessie L. Weston's book on the Grail legend: *From Ritual to Romance*" — a sentence I've stolen the shape of, the way the Kid steals a shirt off a line to have something dry to ride in. Movement VI's epigraph, "Gospel of Jessie James," isn't a joke about the outlaw, whatever it looks like at a glance. It's Weston with a fake mustache on. Make of that what you will. The horse doesn't care whose name is on the bridle.

## On method

These notes aren't decoration, and I'd ask a reader to take that seriously even where the voice gets loose. They're Poundian in the ideogrammic sense — you set one thing down beside another and trust the current to jump the gap, same as Pound sets Malatesta next to Confucius next to a usurer's ledger in the *Cantos* and dares you to call it incoherent. "Dichten = condensare," he wrote, poetry as language charged with meaning to the very limit of what it can carry. A note, done honestly, isn't a loosening of that charge. It's a second compression laid down beside the first, so the two of them throw a shadow neither one throws alone.

Where I quote the dead, I quote them straight, in their own words, because paraphrase is theft without even the nerve of theft. Where I lean on Frye, that literature as a whole isn't new material piling up but the same limited stock of old forms reshaped over and over, I do it because this book is arguing, among other things, that the woman on the road, the woman in the garden, the woman behind the counter, and the woman on the billboard aren't four women but one story wearing four masks — and Frye gave me the words for that before I had the nerve to just say it plain. Where I lean on Brooks, that a poem holds its opposites without resolving them, unified not by killing off the conflict but by living inside it, I do it because the alternative is what he called the heresy of paraphrase, believing a poem can get safely boiled down to a thesis. I've tried, the whole way through, not to commit that particular sin, though a man annotating his own book is already halfway up the ladder to the stake.

## On the epigraphs, plainly, once

The classical epigraphs sitting over most of the numbered movements are, per the Programme, the Kid's own fragmentary translations, made *as* he crosses the very ground this book is set in, not laid over it afterward from some scholar's desk. That's not a new trick. It's Pound's, straight up — Canto I doesn't summarize the Odyssey's descent into the underworld, it *translates* it, straight through Andreas Divus' 1538 Latin crib, seams left showing on purpose. The Kid's Georgics fragments are doing the same work. They aren't Virgil quoted at him. They're Virgil coming *through* him, badly, urgent, in the middle of a waste land he didn't choose and can't leave. Whether he's translating it right is a separate question I'll decline to answer here, for reasons that'll be plain enough to any reader who's made it this far and is still wondering why a grown man writes this much commentary on his own book instead of just answering the question straight.

## On Frye's mythoi, since I lean on them without much ceremony below

Frye splits narrative into four modes keyed loosely to the seasons — romance in summer, the quest; tragedy in autumn, the fall; irony and satire in winter, the world come apart; comedy in spring, integration and return. The Kid's arc doesn't sit still in any one of those, and I'd argue, Frye would likely forgive the argument and Brooks would demand I prove it from the text rather than just assert it, so I've tried to, poem by poem, further in, that the refusal is the actual shape of the book. A romance-quest structure, the road, the wandering, the beloved who keeps coming back, run *through* an ironic mode, the collage, the found text, a billboard's "NO FUTURE REFUNDS," without ever quite landing in tragedy's finished fall or comedy's finished return. The Coda doesn't resolve anything. It restates. That's a choice, not a failure of nerve, though I'll grant the two can look identical depending which way you're standing when you look at it.

## On Kinbote, because somebody's going to say it if I don't

I'm not blind to the fact that a man annotating his own invented cowboy's invented heartbreak at this length, all the while insisting on his scholarly distance from the material, is doing something Charles Kinbote also did to Shade's poem in *Pale Fire*, with a good deal less self-awareness than I'm currently managing to perform. I'll say just this: Kinbote was wrong that the poem was secretly about Zembla. I haven't claimed anywhere in here that this book is secretly about me. I'd ask a hostile reader to notice, though, how often "the Kid" and "I" have started running together in this very paragraph, and draw their own conclusions, the way you'd watch a man cross a river and not bother asking whether he came out wet.

## Where this goes deeper than a first pass usually would

I went back through this whole apparatus more than once, because I'd promised myself something exhaustive and come up short the first time through. What I found on the second and third passes changed how I'd frame the whole thing, and I'd rather say so outright than let the revision hide. The "real girl" paradox this book keeps circling has two much older names than mine — see *Notes.md*, "The esoteric spine," for *The Thunder, Perfect Mind* and the Shekhinah in full: a Gnostic voice declaring her own contradictions as one identity, and a Kabbalistic presence permanently in exile, permanently almost home, never quite either. I'd have rather found those before I published the first round of this. I found them in time to say so plainly, which is more than Kinbote ever managed about his own mistakes.

## What follows

Every movement carries its own note, sitting with its epigraph, tracing the particular classical, esoteric, and formal lineage of that movement's method, troubadour convention in V, the Golden Bough's old survivals in VI, the Metaphysical conceit's late and blasphemous grandchild in VIII, and on through the rest. Every cycle, *Cycle Untitled*, *Peel Back the Moon and Expose It*, *Galveston*, *Black River Love*, *There Are Bones In Her Mouth*, *Sonnet(ish)*, *The Last Real Girl*, and III itself, which is a cycle in everything but the name I gave its folder, carries a note on its own internal workings. And every single poem carries a shorter note of its own, a resonance, a source, a tension worth naming and then, more often than you'd think, worth just leaving unresolved, which Brooks would tell you is usually the more honest of the two options anyhow.

I've aimed to be exhaustive without pretending to be complete, which is the only kind of exhaustive worth going after. A complete note would mean I'd finally understood the Kid whole. I haven't. I've just ridden with him longer than most people would.

A word on how these notes read, since I went back and cleaned house on that too: I'd rather a note sound like something said across a kitchen table than something read off a plaque. Obscurity's never been the same achievement as accuracy, and I've tried, across every pass through this material, to keep chasing the second while I'm reaching for the first. Where a source is genuinely disputed, and a fair number of the ones in here are, I've said so plainly in the note itself rather than let the obscurity alone stand in for rigor. A note that impresses without being checkable isn't scholarship. It's just a longer way of making something up, and I'd rather tell you a shorter true thing than a longer pretty one.

— C.S.
