# V. In a Far Country

They're all just hares on a mountain  
Eyes dashing, fast feet, no hearts for staying.  
Chewing up roots & bolt when storms cough.  
I’ve lain too long in the hunter’s hollow.

Once, I drowned quietly in a mulberry dress,  
Waited for whistles in split-rail heat.  
But the wind lifted only rancid smells,  
and a word I won’t carve on the old gum tree.

I broke my mother’s mirror with a horseshoe.  
I burned my letters in the grease of the stove.  
I wore my boots to bed like a goodbye spell.  
The hens watched me leave without clucking once.

Now I walk where the switchgrass sings.  
I’ve seen the ridge where no rabbits run.  
I will build a chapel out of juniper and ash.

---

### Editorial Includability

**Ezra Pound — 8/10**

> Mulberry dress, grease-burned letters, boots in bed: departure becomes material fact.  
> Keep; the final chapel is earned, though it may need one less sacred noun.

**John Gould Fletcher — 9/10**

> The woman’s leaving is made tangible through dress, mirror, stove grease, boots, hens, and switchgrass.  
> Keep it; the chapel rises convincingly because the preceding world has been so concretely abandoned.
