# On Seeing Venus Rise Above Algiers Point

Your waters fall eastward.  
Mine rise out of the mud.

I saw again that one bright planet  
falling against the rainfresh day.  
You are always early to rise,  
always leaning out into what hasn't yet happened.

Oh you still with sleep on your lips,  
you with the braid undone  
do you see it too?  
That blue star climbing the linen sky?  
Would you tell me if so?

I remember  
when you ran the orange  
through the juicer  
  without the lid—  
    spray  
      on the counter,  
        your shirt,  
          your lip.  
We drank  
the juice together  
  and smiled.

Dream you now, as my senses tell,  
of birds wheeling in an painted sky?  
Do moonbeams pierce your drowsy garden?

I dream of cattle—  
  lowing in fog,  
  bells,  
    fires not yet lit,  
    boots waiting at the door.

Still, each of us turns toward a glowing something  
you, a lamp behind gauze curtains;  
I, to reddened coals

We do not share sleep.  
Our breaths mist mirrored mountains.  
Our rivers speak in foreign tongues  
but still, they flood alike.

---

**1. The morning star and the evening star, which turned out to be one planet all along**

Venus rises and sets under two different names, Hesperus and Phosphorus, and it took the ancient world a good while to work out they were the same body. Sappho gives us the clearest antecedent in what the scholars have termed fragment 104a: *Hesperus, you bring together everything that shining Dawn scattered.* 

**2. A nun who dreamed the same distance**

Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz wrote the nearly thousand-line dream-vision Primero sueño out of a convent in Mexico: the sleeping mind reaching after total knowledge and failing, exhausted, right at the edge of dawn. The poem ends:

el mundo iluminado, y yo despierta.

—the world illuminated, and I awake.

This poem's smaller reach toward someone else across a gap of hours is a gentler cousin to her earlier one consciousness trying to imagine what another sees in sleep.
