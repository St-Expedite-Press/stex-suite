# With Listening Heart

A father lifts a fish, a silver blade,  
a flaring coin bright against the skies,  
its gills, thin-stitched in red, bright seams unmade.

The child cries out, startled, elated,  
wades forward, feet pressing moon-pale shapes  
into wet sand’s palm. Fingers hover,  
hesitate, then reach,  
for the thing that glistens,  
mouth working soundlessly,  
a gulping O.

His mother shades her eyes, the wind unties  
Her loosened hair, a thought unpinned, is sown.  
She watches, lips apart, as if surprised.

My listening heart beats quiet, waits all alone,  
moves with the pull of light upon the sea,  
folds like the tide, unfastened from the stone.

I lift my hand, let fall again  
Unsure what's mine and what has been.

---

### Editorial Includability

**Ezra Pound — 7/10**

> Fish as blade and coin, child’s gulping O: the eye wakes and the poem wakes with it.  
> Keep; end before the final explanatory couplet or make that couplet less decorous.

**John Gould Fletcher — 8/10**

> The family at the shore is observed with tenderness, and the fish flashes through the poem like awakened consciousness.  
> Keep it, but let the tidal uncertainty conclude without the final couplet’s formal tidiness.
