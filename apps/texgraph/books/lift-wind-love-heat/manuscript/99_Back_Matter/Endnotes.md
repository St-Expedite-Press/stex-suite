## Front Matter


### Title Page

**1. Why "No. 1," and not just "a symphony"**

I mean the joke to land with a straight face — no key signature governs a word, I'm aware of that — but the numbering matters more than the key ever could. Beethoven's own First, back in 1800, is the least Beethoven-sounding thing he wrote, still wearing Haydn's hand-me-down coat. A composer's first symphony has always been treated as the proof a bigger form is reachable, not the argument itself. Calling this book a *first* symphony, and not just *a* symphony, commits me to saying everything in it is still green next to something larger the Kid hasn't ridden into yet. I'll stand behind that. I couldn't tell you what the something larger is.

**2. Where the title actually hides**

*Lift Wind/Love Heat* never gets stated as a thesis anywhere in these pages. It shows up exactly once, buried mid-book inside a want ad's found language in movement III, which means a reader meets this book's own name as a stranger's throwaway phrase before they ever learn it's the title at all. Pound liked to say the natural object is always the adequate symbol. Here the natural object is a line of classified copy, handed the whole book to govern without so much as a ceremony. Whether that's democratic or just careless, I'll leave to a harsher critic than myself.

**3. The book's closest living kin**

I don't bring this comparison in lightly, and I've saved it for the title page on purpose, because it isn't a debt the way Virgil or Sappho are debts further in — it's a kinship, and kinship is a more dangerous thing to claim than influence. Frank Stanford wrote a fifteen-thousand-line epic, *The Battlefield Where the Moon Says I Love You*, no stanza breaks, barely any punctuation, narrated by a twelve-year-old orphan-seer named Francis moving through a dream-logic Arkansas and Mississippi that treats the rural South as fully capable of myth as anywhere Homer ever pointed a hexameter. It's the closest thing American letters has to what this book is reaching for at a fraction of the size. Stanford died at twenty-nine, three shots, his own hand, right after confessing an affair to his wife, and the cult that grew up around his poems afterward has never once managed to pull the work loose from the ending. I don't want this book's own Kid read that same uncritical way, a thin mask with the real face showing right through it. Stanford's mask was thin. I'd like to believe this one has more daylight built between the mask and the man. I know good and well that every writer who says that turns out, at least a little, to be wrong.


### Epigraph

**1. Who Verhaeren was, and why I didn't pick a gentler quote**

Verhaeren wrote *La Belgique sanglante* — "Bleeding Belgium" — in the first year of the German occupation, and it isn't really a poem, it's closer to a pamphlet, grief and testimony set down while the grieving was still happening. "The work of so many years is annihilated; the hopes of yesterday are dead" wasn't a figure of speech for him. He was a Belgian looking at an actually ruined Belgium. He didn't live to see how it ended, either — died in 1916, hit by a train at the station in Rouen, trying to catch it same as anybody. I think about that a good deal, honestly. A man spends a war writing about what can't be undone and then a train, of all the ordinary things, is what gets him.

I put this at the front of a book about a made-up wasteland because I think the real one is the made-up one's grandfather. Verhaeren's 1915 is Eliot's *Waste Land* seven years early, same ground, same rubble, and the line between them runs straight through the trenches whether or not either man ever shook the other's hand. I know good and well that borrowing a real country's grief to preface a story about a cowboy's broken heart is a risky thing to do — no footnote fully earns that back. I left it here anyway, because "guard, with courage and patience, what remains" is about the only instruction this whole book actually manages to follow all the way through.

**2. The Symbolist logic underneath the borrowing**

Verhaeren came up inside French Symbolism, and Symbolism's whole working theory of the world — inherited from Swedenborg's doctrine of *correspondences*, the notion that everything visible answers to something spiritual standing behind it — is the same idea Baudelaire put into verse in "Correspondances": *"La Nature est un temple où de vivants piliers / Laissent parfois sortir de confuses paroles"* — nature as a temple where the living pillars let slip a confused word now and then, if you're listening right. A book that keeps holding Belgium's real ruin up next to a fictional man's private one is practicing that same doctrine whether it knows the name for it or not — trusting that one kind of loss can answer another without the two ever needing to become the same loss.

That the epigraph arrives in French, ahead of a book set mostly along the Gulf and through Louisiana, isn't incidental either. French down here has done double duty as the language of loss and the language of holding on for going on three centuries — Cajun and Creole French didn't survive in spite of everything working against it, it survived *because* people kept speaking it defiantly, past every law and every schoolyard slap that told them to quit. Verhaeren wasn't writing for Louisiana. But the posture travels.

**3. Why the jarring is the point, not a flaw**

Setting a poet of industrial catastrophe and national ruin at the head of what's otherwise a pastoral, personal book is a rough transition, and I didn't smooth it over on purpose. The private losses that follow get placed under the sign of a real historical one without ever being told they're equal to it. That distinction is the whole reason for the choice. I'd rather a reader feel the seam than have me sand it down for them.


### Preface

**1. Ovid sent his book out ahead of him, same as this one goes out ahead of me**

"Go, little one" is the oldest version we've got of a poet putting his book on the road without him. Ovid, stuck out at Tomis on the edge of everything Rome cared about, opens the *Tristia* the same way: *Parve — nec invideo — sine me, liber, ibis in urbem: / ei mihi, quod domino non licet ire tuo!* — Little book, go on without me, I don't begrudge it, to the city; but ah, that your own master isn't allowed to go. Chaucer borrowed the same gesture centuries later to close out *Troilus and Criseyde*, "Go, litel bok, go, litel myn tragedye." By the time it lands in this preface, it's less a clever nod and more just the only honest way a man's found to say it: I can't follow this where it's going, so it'll have to carry me.

Ovid's exile was a real: he never got back to Rome. The *Tristia* is as close as he ever came again. This preface borrows all that ache for a narrator who, as far as the book ever tells you, was never exiled from anywhere in particular — which either hollows the whole gesture out, or proves, if you take Frye's reading of myth seriously, that exile doesn't need a real biography behind it to be a true shape a life can take. I lean toward the second. I would, though, wouldn't I.

**2. What it means to ask a reader to receive a book rather than just read it**

The *Corpus Hermeticum*'s first tractate, *Poimandres*, frames the whole revelation it delivers as conditional — only "whoever has received a portion of this gift" gets the thing entire, and everybody else is free to walk on by. "If there is yet one among the throng who recalls me... let him look upon you, / let him read / or, if he must, scorn you" is working that same old logic. This book isn't out here asking to be understood by everybody. It's asking to be found by whoever it was already halfway meant for, and it's granting everybody else full permission to put it down without hard feelings.


## Overture


### Overture (section epigraph)

**1. The one psalm that never turns the corner**

O LORD GOD OF MY SALVATION, I HAVE CRIED DAY AND NIGHT BEFORE THEE is Psalm 88 lifted whole, and 88 is worth knowing exactly for what it refuses. Every other complaint psalm eventually turns toward hope. Eighty eight does not. It just ends in darkness and stops. A drunk, feversung saloon psalm reaching for the one psalm that never turns the corner is not a small choice, and it tells you what the Kid is actually praying before he has done a single thing in this book.

**2. No epigraph, because there is no country to translate yet**

Every other movement in this book carries a classical fragment the Kid has, by the Programme's own logic, translated crossing the ground he is standing on. You cannot translate a country you have not crossed. The Overture is where he arrives, so it gets no epigraph, the same way the Coda gets none at the far end. Fittingly, the valley this Wailing Pit is standing in for is a real one, the Valley of Hinnom outside old Jerusalem, once the site of actual atrocity and later shorthand for judgment itself, Gehenna. A saloon borrowing that old weight for ordinary sin is just this culture reaching for the vocabulary it never built a fresher version of.


### The Kid & The Wailing Pit

**1. A hell with a house band**

"The gulches, in darkness — down deeps — / there are kings, borders of lands where none breathe, / the lowest pits, the tombs" is Dante's country before it's the Kid's — the *Malebolge* of *Inferno* XVIII, ringed evil-ditches, each one holding its own particular crowd of the damned. Dante needed Virgil to walk him down through it. The Kid's got a saloon piano and a preacher who'll "wager his faith on games of cards," which is a real old American type, the gambler-preacher, half con man and half true believer, and he doesn't guide anybody anywhere. He's just the house band for wherever this is.

**2. The one psalm that never turns the corner**

"O LORD GOD OF MY SALVATION, / I HAVE CRIED DAY & NIGHT BEFORE THEE" isn't the Kid's own words dressed up as scripture — it's Psalm 88, verse one, lifted whole. And Psalm 88 is worth knowing for what it refuses to do: every other psalm of complaint eventually turns, finds its way to "yet will I trust in him," some kind of hope by the last line. Eighty-eight doesn't. It just ends in darkness and quits. A drunk, feversung "saloon psalm" reaching for the one psalm in the whole book that never once turns the corner — that's not a small choice on this book's part. It tells you exactly what the Kid's actually praying, whether he could name the psalm or not.

**3. A door with a name to it**

There's an old apocryphal text, the *Testament of Solomon*, where the king binds a run of demons with a magic ring and makes each one say its own name and what it does before he'll let it go — the idea being a thing named right is a thing you've got some hold on. "Known to wager his faith on games of cards" gives this poem's preacher that same old method, minus the ring, minus the hold. He's still doing the naming, psalm after psalm. He just lost whatever used to come after.


## I. Vibrations of the Dented Spheres


### I. Vibrations of the Dented Spheres (section epigraph)

**1. Harmony, dented on purpose**

The title borrows from Pythagoras, the old harmony of the spheres, later sorted by Boethius into three orders, the cosmos, the soul, and what your own ear can actually catch. Anaxagoras got prosecuted in Athens for saying the sun was just a hot rock rather than a god, centuries before this movement dented the same spheres its own way, keeping both possibilities open, still capable of harmony, still visibly banged up.

**2. A book that opens by disclaiming its own originality**

The movement's own epigraph, it aint about right, or new, just let the story find its tune, is the one unborrowed ars poetica in the whole book, and it is deliberately anti-Poundian, refusing to make it new right out of the gate. What follows is a hard landing, two poems in an elevated cosmic register followed by three flat, contemporary ones, a poem about a Bluetooth pairing request among them. A book this stacked with borrowed classical apparatus opening by shrugging off originality as a value at all has told you something true about its whole shape before movement two even starts.


### Harmonikum

**1. Music of the spheres, before it got dented**

The title's borrowing straight from Pythagoras — *musica universalis*, the harmony of the spheres, which Boethius later sorted into three kinds: the music of the cosmos, the music of the soul's own proportion, and the music you can actually put your ear to. Kepler still believed the planets sang real intervals as late as 1619. This poem doesn't believe it that plainly. It believes it *dented* — the spheres are still turning, the tune's still up there somewhere, but something's knocked against it, and the poem admits as much straight out: "the gods wait — still, serene, unheard." That's Sappho's whole ache in fragment 31 turned cosmic — a mortal ear straining after an order it can only pick up as static.

**2. The oldest formula for one thing becoming many and staying one**

There's an axiom credited to Maria Prophetissa, one of the earliest alchemists on record, that comes down to us secondhand through later writers quoting her: *one becomes two, two becomes three, and out of the third comes the one as the fourth.* How much of that's really her own words and how much is later paraphrase, nobody can say for certain. But it's kin to this poem's "seven voices rising in song, their golden echoes gleaming / Each reflecting the next, a circle of sound and light" — multiplying outward and still somehow circling home. Jung took the axiom seriously enough to read it as a map of the whole process of becoming a self. This poem doesn't get that far. It just watches the circle turn and admits, by the last line, that the god at the center's gone quiet.


### On Seeing Venus Rise Above Algiers Point

**1. The morning star and the evening star, which turned out to be one planet all along**

Venus rises and sets under two different names, Hesperus and Phosphorus, and it took the ancient world a good while to work out they were the same body. Sappho already knew what to do with the doubling in fragment 104a: *Hesperus, you bring together everything that shining Dawn scattered.* This whole poem runs on that same logic — one lover always rising early "into what hasn't happened yet," the other still reaching for "reddened coals," two people who "do not share sleep," one planet wearing two names depending what hour you catch it.

**2. A nun who dreamed the same distance**

Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz wrote a nearly thousand-line dream-vision, *Primero sueño*, out of a convent in Mexico in 1692 — the mind reaching after total knowledge and failing, exhausted, right at the edge of dawn. She was the most learned woman in the Americas at the time, and her own church made her sell her library and quit writing a few years after. This poem's small reach toward someone else across a gap of hours is a gentler cousin to her larger one. Worth remembering, next time a poem this hopeful gets waved off as merely romantic, that the last woman who wrote this particular kind of longing was eventually made to stop, in writing, over her own signature.


### Rules!

**1. The mark of Cain, read the other way**

"He looked at the Kid / like God looked at Cain / in the moments before the mark" is worth slowing down on, because the mark in Genesis 4 wasn't extra punishment — it was protection, God's own guarantee that nobody would kill Cain for what he'd already done. There was even a minor Gnostic sect, the Cainites, that Irenaeus wrote against around 180 CE, who venerated Cain outright, on the theory that the god who'd cursed him wasn't the real one to be listening to. I won't claim this poem's arguing theology that fine-grained. But "he looked at the Kid like God looked at Cain" reads a lot closer to a man about to be marked *safe* than a man about to be condemned.

**2. The rulebook's own crack**

I'll say plainly what I skipped past too easy the first time through: "I know all the rules. Sit with it and be uncomfortable" is the flattest line in this movement, a therapy-office cadence dropped into a poem that otherwise reaches for Cain and marked men. I considered smoothing the seam. I didn't, because a poem's allowed two centuries of diction in one mouth if that's honestly where the man's voice is standing at that moment — but I'll own that it's a real seam, not a subtle one.


### Who's There?

**1. A city where the water and the door both remember**

"Twenty six zero six Royal Street" sits close enough to a real, documented practice that I don't think the closeness is accidental: after Katrina, search teams spray-painted an X on flooded doors, four quadrants recording the date searched, who searched it, what they found, how many dead inside. A door described as "smeared the color of a wound / almost healed," on a flooded street, has already worn a mark like that once and never fully stopped wearing it.

**2. The house of sirens, and the real queen down the block**

"House of the Sirens" reaches back to Homer, the *Odyssey*'s promise of total knowledge to any sailor who'll quit rowing and just listen, and the death that promise costs him. But Royal Street carries its own local ghost too — Marie Laveau's own reputation still runs through those blocks a hundred and forty years after her death, healing and hexing and controlling weather all mixed in with whatever the actual woman did or didn't do. A poem naming this exact stretch of street is standing on ground that's never fully stopped being read as a threshold between the ordinary and whatever's on the other side of it.


### Pairing Request

**1. The old courtship token, traded in for a Bluetooth handshake**

Courtly love ran on objects — a glove, a ring, something worn into battle that let two people claim a bond the social order wouldn't otherwise let them state outright. This poem's token is a pairing request. "New device detected / would you like to remember this location?" is asking for the same permission a medieval token asked for, just wearing different hardware. "No. / (yes.) / (always.)" is the old courtly *yes* smuggled inside a public *no* — same shape it's always had, just running on a different battery.


## II. Chansons des terres battues


### II. Chansons des terres battues (section epigraph)

*Chansons des terres battues* means "songs of the beaten earth". This is the *pastourelle* register, the medieval French lyric of the open field and the working land, but crossed here with the Mississippi Delta rather than the Île-de-France — a graft Virgil's own epigraph licenses, since the Georgics are themselves poems about *particular dirt*, not dirt in general: "trees are dealt out their countries," each soil answering to one crop and no other. The Delta poems that follow take that literally.

"To Myself, A Fevered Fool" tips its hand in its subtitle — "A Cameo From Catullus" — and the model is exactly Catullus 8, *Miser Catulle, desinas ineptire*, the poem where the poet turns and addresses himself by name, orders himself to stop being a fool over a woman who's already gone: *quae tu numquam amabis* — "she whom you will never love again." The Kid's version keeps the self-address, drops the resolve. Catullus at least manages the imperative mood. The Kid only manages the complaint.

The two additions to this movement's original run — "Blues on A Sunday Night," "Cold, Cold Again" — are New Orleans poems set down inside a movement titled for open, beaten, agricultural ground, and the friction is intentional rather than smoothed over: a city built on drained swamp is still, underneath the concrete, land that had to be *dealt out* its own impossible country, per Virgil's line above. The rattling trash cans are just what "no soil bears all things" sounds like once you've paved it.

*i never worked a field in my life. don't let that fool you. every road i've ridden was somebody's fence line first.*

### An Old Man

**1. A stillness that isn't waiting for anything**

Thomas Traherne wrote almost entirely in obscurity — his *Centuries of Meditations* wasn't published till 1908, found by accident on a London bookstall, nearly lost for good. His whole subject was learning to see ordinary stillness as already whole, not a pause before something better. "Nothing displaces him" is Traherne's discipline in four words. A man who's quit waiting for the world to rearrange itself hasn't given up. He's just gotten there early.

**2. Watching without explaining**

This is deep-image work, plain and simple — the mode James Wright and Robert Bly worked in mid-century, where a still natural scene carries the feeling without ever being asked to explain itself. Wright ends "A Blessing" on "I would break / into blossom" with nothing behind it but two ponies in a field. This poem trusts "one twig snaps" the same way. Nobody tells you the old man's dignified. You're just shown he doesn't move when the squirrels do.


### East of the Pine Line

**1. Pound's own rule, followed to the letter**

Pound's imagist commandments — direct treatment of the thing, no word that doesn't earn its place — govern this poem about as strictly as anything in the whole book. Three couplets, nothing decorative, and the feeling's carried entirely by what the inanimate things are doing: the heron "ain't got no lift," the porch light shows "no mercy," the wind "knows better / than to knock." That's the same refusal to explain that makes "In a Station of the Metro" work — you're shown the wind won't knock. Nobody tells you why.

**2. A light left burning that's forgotten how to stop**

Leaving a light on through the night to guard a threshold is about as old and widespread a folk habit as there is — meant to tell the living and the dead both that somebody's still watching. "The porch light's fixed now, / always on... no blinking, / no mercy" takes that old comfort and turns it exhausting: a vigil that's outlasted whatever it was keeping watch for.


### Blues on A Sunday Night

**1. Repetition that never resolves**

Charley Patton cut "High Water Everywhere" in 1929, a firsthand account of the 1927 Mississippi flood told through the same phrase circling back on itself till it carries more weight than any single telling could. "Rattle, rattle, rattle" is doing that exact trick at a much smaller scale — civic neglect standing in for Patton's flood, both trusting repetition to do the work plain description can't.

**2. The charm that shrinks as it works**

There's a real Roman charm, attributed to the physician Serenus Sammonicus, that had fever sufferers wear ABRACADABRA written eleven times in a shrinking triangle, one letter dropped each line, the sickness supposed to vanish right along with the word. This poem's own repetition doesn't shrink. It just keeps rattling the same length, over and over — either a charm that's failing to work, or one that was never going to end.


### Cold, Cold Again

**1. A borrowed season, and the older story about why it's late**

Demeter's bargain with Hades is the oldest excuse Western myth's got for a season showing up wrong — six months of her daughter gone bought six months of winter, ready or not. "It's spring now. / Or it should be spring" carries a small, secular echo of that same old complaint, a culture that once needed a story for why the seasons don't always keep their appointments.

**2. The lights that lead you nowhere good**

Louisiana Cajun folklore keeps its own version of the will-o'-the-wisp, the *feux follets* — spirits of the restless dead, wandering lights said to lead a traveler into the marsh and leave him there. Nobody in this poem gets literally led anywhere. But the shape of being drawn toward a light that won't deliver what it promises, calico cats sitting still while the season insists on a warmth it isn't actually offering, belongs to the *feux follets* whether the poem names them or not.


### Cycle Untitled (cycle)

**1. Grief that keeps breaking its own surface**

Italicized fragments keep interrupting Angry Delta Sky mid-line, catfish, his eyes, the river again, and once, chansons, the movement's own title word surfacing inside the poem it titles, like a man overhearing his own name through a wall. That is close kin to what Anne Carson does typographically with damaged Sappho papyrus, the gap itself made to carry meaning.

**2. Unnamed on purpose**

A Greek tragic chorus does not need a name to carry weight, its anonymity is part of how it speaks for more than one household. This cycle stays Untitled the same way, refusing to name the couple at its center because Frye's romance mode wants a named quest object and this is not that, only the shape a great many rural marriages take, worn smooth of any one particular identity by the same erosion the poems describe.


### Cycle Untitled — I. That's How It Is

**1. A marriage told through objects, not statements**

"They love like creekbeds love drought — quiet, cracked open, full of jammed logs" opens the cycle in Raymond Carver territory, marriage rendered through habit and object rather than declared feeling, a screen door "telling the truth" neither spouse will say straight out. The simile does the whole job by itself: love compared not to fire or flood, but to what a creekbed does *without* water — just holds its shape and waits.


### Cycle Untitled — II. Angry Delta Sky

**1. A flood this poem is standing in the memory of**

This poem never names 1927, but it doesn't need to. The Great Mississippi Flood that year drowned a stretch of land the size of New England and displaced something like one percent of the country's whole population, and its failures in levee policy are still shaping how people talk about the Delta a century later. "The heavens spilt their guts in the flooded fields" reads, against that history, less like invented apocalypse and more like inherited memory — a regional wound big enough it doesn't need restating to still be what's being described.

**2. The italics that break the poem's own surface**

Fragments keep interrupting this poem mid-line — *catfish*, *his eyes*, *the river again*, *my hand*, and once, *chansons*, the movement's own title-word surfacing inside the poem it titles, like a man overhearing his own name through a wall. It's close kin to what Anne Carson does typographically with damaged Sappho papyrus, the gap itself made to carry meaning. This poem's grief keeps breaking its own surface the same way. That's not an accident of formatting. That's just what grief does to a sentence.


### Cycle Untitled — III. Silvish

**1. A ghost who hasn't shown up yet**

Old ballads give us two different shapes for a dead woman come back. "The Unquiet Grave" has her rise to tell her grieving lover to stop, that his mourning's keeping her from resting. "The Wife of Usher's Well" gives three drowned sons one single night home before cockcrow forces them back down. This poem's ghost hasn't arrived at all yet, only promised — "my love is a ghost says she won't haunt me yet" — which leaves its speaker in neither ballad's position, gun in hand, still waiting on the one night he isn't even sure he's owed.


### To Myself, A Fevered Fool

**1. Catullus, minus the resolve**

The subtitle points straight at Catullus 8, *Miser Catulle, desinas ineptire* — the poem where he turns and orders himself, by name, to stop being a fool over a woman already gone. This version keeps the self-address and drops the order. Catullus at least manages the imperative mood. This poem only manages the complaint.

**2. What a curse tablet promises that Catullus doesn't**

Greek and Roman *defixiones* — thin lead sheets, inscribed and buried or thrown down a well — survive by the thousands, and a good share of them aren't curses so much as love-binding spells: compel this person to want no one else. This poem's confidence that "not even Jove himself" could compete sits closer to a *defixio*'s certainty than to Catullus's self-command. It isn't asking to be hardened against her. It's just stating, flat out, that nothing could outbid what it's already lost to.


### Moan

**1. A murder told the way a ballad tells it**

No version of "Stagger Lee" — the American ballad that grew up out of a real 1895 St. Louis murder — asks you to feel sorry for anybody in it. The shooting gets reported, the hat gets noted, the song moves on. "His icy glyph spewed no doubt with glee... i knocked he didn't speak but pulled a chair" works that same withheld sympathy, a scene that would demand outrage in any other register, told here flat, like a man just telling you what he saw.


## IV. The Gods Touch Flesh


### IV. The Gods Touch Flesh (section epigraph)

**1. Real coats, not costumes**

Every piece in this movement has a real coat to wear. Be No Longer Troubled and A Goddess Is No Widow run the Diana and Endymion myth, and the Latin interleaved through the first one is not decoration, it is the fossil record of the translation happening in real time, the same seam Pound left showing in Canto I. After the Little Eyes declares its debt outright to Propertius, and He Is Possessed By Absence is not generic troubadour longing, it is amor de lonh exactly, the love-from-a-distance Jaufre Rudel built his whole surviving body of work on.

**2. A body that stops working right, and a god you are left to infer**

Fragment From Sappho closes the movement on fragment 31 in close paraphrase, the trembling, the greener than grass, the voice that no longer comes, which Longinus quoted whole as the model of passion rendered through physical collapse rather than statement. None of these poems tell you a god touched flesh. The body just stops working right, and John's own line, the Word was made flesh, sits close by as the one tradition that insists the touching was total and for keeps. These gods touch flesh and let go instead.


### Peel Back the Moon and Expose It (cycle)

**1. A pronoun that revises the whole myth**

Be No Longer Troubled addresses the Moon as you, pleading. A Goddess Is No Widow answers back as I, refusing the plea's whole premise. Most versions of this myth, Lyly's, Keats's, have the goddess gaze and the shepherd sleep, sympathy sitting with her longing because he never gets to say a word. Giving the silent half of an old myth a first person voice is this cycle's whole argument, and the pronoun shift is where you can watch it happen.

**2. Madness, and where the word for it actually comes from**

Lunaticus, of the moon, was formal medical and legal language for centuries, lunacy stayed on the books in English law into the twentieth century. Peel back the moon and expose it treats the moon as something with an interior worth uncovering, closer to interrogating the old diagnosis than suffering under it.


### Peel Back the Moon — Be No Longer Troubled

**1. A prisoner comforted the same way**

Boethius wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison, awaiting execution, Lady Philosophy appearing to steady him with reasoned argument set partly in verse. Why howl you, Moon runs that same shape, one voice trying to calm another through direct address rather than argument alone. Boethius was executed not long after finishing the book. Whether his own philosophy actually consoled him at the end, the text itself never says.

**2. A cultic address, not a decorative one**

The Orphic Hymns, a real devotional collection used in actual ritual, include a hymn to Selene asking her to shine a kindly light on rites already underway by moonlight. This poem addresses the moon in that same cultic register, not decoration, closer to petitioning an attending god than describing a symbol.


### Peel Back the Moon — A Goddess Is No Widow

**1. Another woman defending her own authority, unasked**

The Gospel of Mary, a Coptic text likely from the second century, stages Peter openly doubting Marys authority to have received a private vision, with another apostle, Levi, defending her against him. I have danced, I have whirled, through the bluesky arcades is not aimed at any accuser this poem bothers to name, but the goddess speaking those lines has learned something Marys defenders in that gospel had not yet needed to: the doubt does not require an audience to be worth refusing.

**2. The pronoun that revises the whole myth**

Lyly and Keats both gave English poetry an Endymion where the moon-goddess gazes and the shepherd sleeps, sympathy sitting by convention with her longing because he never gets to say anything at all. This cycles second panel breaks that convention outright, she speaks, at length, and what she says is not grief but something closer to boredom dressed as dignity. Be No Longer Troubled addresses the Moon as you, pleading. A Goddess Is No Widow answers as I, refusing the plea's premise entirely. The pronoun shift is the myth's revision, done in one word.


### Peel Back the Moon — Untitled Loss

**1. Actaeon, and the horned god standing just behind him**

The stag that sees me not, watching an empty sky, reaches for Actaeon without naming him, Ovids hunter who stumbles on Diana bathing and is turned into a stag for the accident of seeing her, then torn apart by his own hounds. Behind Actaeon stands an older, gentler figure, Cernunnos, the horned Celtic lord of animals depicted on the Gundestrup Cauldron, seated calm among his creatures rather than punished among them. This stag answers to neither god fully, too passive for Actaeons myth, too wounded for Cernunnoss company.


### After the Little Eyes

**1. Propertius's whole method, felt from inside the campaign**

"Crashing out Like Sextus Propertius" declares the debt outright, and the Roman elegist's formal complaint against a woman's fickleness, his querela, is bone-deep in this poem, compare Propertius's own opening line, Cynthia first captured wretched me with her eyes, against I foresaw the many bitter shapes your wanton levity might one day wear. Ovid called love a soldier's business in the Amores, his own camp, his own campaigns, his own wounds. This poem reads like the after-action report, damage catalogued with the same weary precision.

**2. The old fear that a look alone could wound you**

After the Little Eyes leans, whether it means to or not, into the malocchio, the evil eye, a belief that ran the length of the ancient and medieval Mediterranean, that a glance, envious or admiring, could genuinely do harm. Propertius never needed the folklore, his accusation was always that the eyes did something, not that they were merely looked into. This poem keeps that conviction and just lets the older superstition hum quietly underneath it.


### In Response, A Sonnet

**1. The old belief about how you bring an elephant down**

Medieval bestiaries held that elephants slept leaning against trees, having no knee joints to speak of, and that hunters would saw a tree half through and let the beast fall, unable to rise on its own again. Watch hunters draw the elephant to die leans on that old, wrong bit of natural history for its whole octave, a creature undone by its own resting posture, which this sonnet uses as a figure for erotic ruin, falling not as accident, but as a trap nobody warned you the ground itself was built to be.

**2. A woman who wrote passion without the usual hedge**

Gaspara Stampa, generally reckoned the finest woman poet of the Italian Renaissance, wrote desire straight, without the decorous suffering her male contemporaries hid behind. This sonnets closing couplet, do not lie you love him most when he will not die, has some of that same nerve, an admission most courtly convention would rather leave unsaid than confess to outright.


### Sonnet To She Who Blames The Stars

**1. Shakespeare already argued this one out**

Not from the stars do I my judgement pluck, Shakespeare wrote in Sonnet 14, and this sonnet takes the harder road, conceding the stars might as well take the blame, then pulling it back at the last line: accuse one star, the cold one in thy heart. Sidney built his whole Astrophil and Stella on the same pun sitting quiet in his heros name, star-lover. This poem plays the pun straight rather than clever.

**2. When blaming the stars was actual medicine, not metaphor**

Medieval and early modern doctors mapped the whole body onto the zodiac, the Zodiac Man, Aries at the skull down through Pisces at the feet, printed in every physicians almanac for centuries. Blaming the stars for a love affair gone wrong was not only poetic back then, a lot of people meant it clinically. This sonnets insistence that the blame belongs to the cold one in thy heart instead is arguing against the actual medical consensus of whatever century it has landed in, not just against a figure of speech.


### That Other Moon

**1. A book most scholars distrust and most poets cannot quit**

Robert Graves argued in The White Goddess that nearly all true poetry descends from one prehistoric moon-goddess cult, three-formed as maiden, mother, crone. Folklorists mostly treat the book as inspired invention rather than real history, and I will not pretend otherwise. But that triple goddess, cycling shape without ever stopping being one thing, gave two generations of poets a working vocabulary for exactly what this poem needs: the moon, her only meter, tilts slightly, over all. Whether Graves found an old truth or just built a beautiful one, this poems moon is closer kin to his than to any astronomers.


### With Listening Heart

**1. The titles own source, almost word for word**

This title is close to a direct translation of 1 Kings 3:9, Solomons prayer for lev shomea, literally a hearing heart, usually rendered in English as an understanding heart, the request that made him wise. Old Near Eastern wisdom writing keeps putting comprehension in the ear rather than the eye, you understand by hearing, obediently, not by simply observing. This whole poem is built out of watching, a father, a fish, a childs reaching hand, and yet it names itself for the one sense none of that watching involves. The heart is doing the listening the eyes cannot manage.

**2. Keeping the heart, not just the eyes, on watch**

The Desert Fathers held that the heart, not the mind, was the true seat of attention, prayer meant to travel down out of the head and settle, wordless, in the chest. My listening heart beats quiet, waits all alone is reaching for that same relocation, away from what the eyes are busy doing and down into an organ the poem trusts to understand what watching by itself never could.


### He Is Possessed By Absence

**1. Love from a country you will never actually reach**

This is not troubadour convention in general, it is amor de lonh exactly, the love-from-a-distance Jaufre Rudel built his whole surviving body of work around in the twelfth century: love from a far-off land, for you my whole heart aches. Rudel is supposed to have died in the arms of a woman he had loved for years without ever meeting her. This poem never confirms whether its own speaker has met his distances or not, which strikes me as the more honest position to leave open.

**2. What astrology calls it when closeness erases you**

There is a real technical term in old astrology, combustion, when a planet sits too close to the sun to be seen at all, its light simply overwhelmed by the nearness. Distance, in that vocabulary, is what lets a thing be seen. Nearness is what erases it. This poems whole ache runs on that same inversion, getting closer would not save this love, it would just put the light out.


### Eurydice and the Snake

**1. Virgils snake, not Ovids**

The serpent belongs to Virgil first, Georgics IV, not the Aeneid, where a snake in the grass at Eurydices fleeing feet is the one small accident that costs Orpheus everything his music cannot undo. This book has already leaned on three other books of the Georgics for its epigraphs. This one just shows up inside the poem instead, uncredited, which either means the well ran a little dry or means some debts read better left inside the verse than announced over it.

**2. Rilke gave her the myth back, and this poem will not**

Rilkes Orpheus. Eurydice. Hermes. retells the myth from her side entirely, and by the time Orpheus turns to look, she is already so reconciled to being dead, she was already root, that being led back to life would be its own kind of violence. This poem stays at the bite instead, jaw opens like it has always been about to strike, which keeps a wound open that Rilke, more mercifully, let close.


### He Draws the Seven of Swords

**1. Where the cards actual image came from**

Before 1908, tarots plain numbered suits were almost never illustrated with any scene at all, just repeated symbols. That year, black and white photographs of the 1491 Sola Busca deck, the earliest fully illustrated tarot on record, went up at the British Museum, the same year Pamela Colman Smith started designing the deck everybody now calls Rider Waite. For nearly a dozen cards, including this one, her imagery comes recognizably from that five hundred year old engraving, rediscovered just in time to land in front of the one illustrator who needed it.

**2. Victory that costs more than it is worth**

Golden Dawn correspondence assigns the Seven of Swords to Netzach, Victory, in Air, and glosses it as unstable effort, a win that costs more to hold onto than it was worth taking. That instability sits right under this poems own hedge: for now, for ever. The card does not promise the getaway holds. It only promises the getting away felt, for one cards worth of time, like winning.


### Light Reaches Last

**1. Dickinson noticed a fly. This poem will not allow one.**

Dickinsons dying speaker in I heard a Fly buzz when I died notices something almost insultingly small right at the end, the sacred interrupted by the ordinary. This old man gets the opposite treatment, nebulae dilating in his lungs, nothing small allowed near him, the motel room in cracked concrete and colored light insisting on cosmic scale straight through the last line. Dickinson trusted the fly to be more honest than the grandeur everyone expected. This poem bets the other way, that a man is owed the grandeur.


### Fragment From Sappho

**1. Fragment 31 is ancient. This poems ending is not.**

Longinus already had fragment 31 whole when he quoted it in On the Sublime, some two thousand years back, the trembling, the greener than grass, the voice that no longer comes. Our closing lines, all things may be dared, even by the pauper, are not part of any surviving fragment. No papyrus gives us that. Sapphos own corpus is still being pieced together in our lifetime, in fact, a major new fragment, the so called Tithonus poem, was not published complete till 2005. This poems addition joins her the same way that discovery did, late, uninvited by the ancient text, and changing how the whole thing reads regardless.


## III. Forward Murder in a Reverse Day in Baton Rouge


### III. Forward Murder in a Reverse Day in Baton Rouge (section epigraph)

**1. A city named for a boundary marker**

Baton Rouge, red stick, was named by early French explorers for a reddened pole marking a boundary between two tribal hunting grounds. A movement about murder and reversed time, set in a city whose own name commemorates an earlier disputed line in the dirt, is standing on ground that has been marking boundaries, and the cost of crossing them, a good while longer than this book's own day takes to unwind.

**2. A day run backward, and knowledge withheld from the man living it**

Five poems, one day, running evening to night, late afternoon, noon, pre-dawn to dawn, origins. The technique, telegram, want ad, static, the refrain lift the wind, love the heat surfacing and going under again, owes more to Dos Passos's Newsreel interludes than to any lyric tradition, found civic language dropped in raw and left to accuse itself. Running the day backward toward origins rather than forward toward consequence means the reader knows more than the poem's own speaker does by the time you reach I. Before the Pulse. That withheld knowledge is the whole engine of the thing.


### V. Last Light Leans

**1. The music that usually escorts the dead here does not show up**

A New Orleans jazz funeral does not separate mourning from music, a slow dirge on the way out and a jubilant second line coming back, the dead sent off and the living reminded to keep living. This poems jukebox needle rides the groove, slows down runs that same procession backward, decline instead of release, the record slowing toward silence with no second line waiting to pick it back up on the way home.


### IV. Window-Unit Dust

**1. The dust this poem is standing downwind of**

Woody Guthrie sang the Dust Bowl as an economy that had stopped providing and dressed its failure up as opportunity anyway. This poems dust is smaller and more domestic, a window unit instead of a whole county, but it works from the identical premise. Must lift the wind, said the want ad, paid by dust is Guthries whole decade compressed into one classified line, and the title phrase of this entire book turns up right here, inside somebody elses job posting, before the reader ever learns it is the title at all.


### III. Noon, What They Don't Say

**1. The comedy that will not stay separate from the horror**

Flannery O Connor never let her violence and her comedy occupy separate sentences, the two sharing one flat register straight through. Job: watch the kids and smoke government grass, rent is in the red, so is the baby plays the want ad for the joke it almost is before landing, one clause later, on the thing that is not funny at all. O Connor would know that timing as her own.


### II. Fridge Buzz

**1. The color of paint this trailer never got**

Gullah and broader Southern building tradition painted porch ceilings haint blue, on the belief that a spirit cannot tell the color from open water and will not cross it, a cheap and durable ward that only needed the right can of paint. This FEMA trailer has no such ceiling, only a fridge buzzing like light dying, unwarded by any color at all, which is one specific way of saying this is emergency housing and not a home, the difference marked by a tradition nobody had time to keep up when the trailer arrived.


### I. Before the Pulse

**1. STOP was never dramatic. It was just cheaper.**

STOP in old telegrams was not the sender being theatrical, telegrams billed by the word and STOP cost nothing where a period sometimes did. A generation also learned to read the mere arrival of a War Department telegram as the message itself, dread arriving before a single word got opened. This poems own telegram, THE SHERIFFS LUNGS, THEY HAVE BEEN TRANSLATED INTO SPANISH, keeps that old dread fully intact while stripping out anything that could pass for sense, a form so tied to bad news it does not need the news to make sense in order to land as bad.


## V. Other Voices, Other Roads


### V. Other Voices, Other Roads (section epigraph)

**1. A form with a real border in its second line, four centuries early**

The serranilla is a real, specific form, not borrowed atmosphere. The Marques de Santillana wrote its surviving classics in fifteenth century Castile, a nobleman riding the sierra undone by a peasant girl, and the border already sits in his second line, long before this movement's own epigraph puts men on one. This movement's title lands close enough to Capote's Other Voices, Other Rooms that I will not claim direct influence I cannot prove, only note it plainly, roads standing in for rooms, identity found on unfamiliar ground rather than at home either way.

**2. Five women, one road, and a prayer never once made**

Homer belongs here because Chryses's plea in the Iliad, a father demanding his daughter back, a prayer for homeward passage, sits over a movement about a man who cannot stop not going home. Five different women appear across these poems, and Frye's romance mode, the quest that keeps circling the same object under new names, is the only frame flexible enough to hold all five without forcing them into one or five. I do not force it either. I just count.


### The Prickly Pear That Spoke To Me

**1. A bush that spoke once before, and a cactus that expects payment**

A talking plant in the desert has one obvious ancestor, Exodus and the burning bush that speaks Moses name and does not consume itself. This poem keeps the desert and drops the covenant. What is left is closer to toloache, jimsonweed, in Mexican and Southwestern curandera tradition, a plant treated as a jealous feminine spirit owed real respect, sometimes blood, in exchange for whatever vision or connection it grants. Sometimes we just want your blood is not a new idea to anybody raised near that plants reputation.


### Galveston (cycle)

**1. A cycle that branches inward instead of outward**

Four panels, four grammatical moods, Knees runs pure future conditional, Blue Hour retreats into counterfactual past, Shotguns layers a second counterfactual on the first, and IV collapses the whole apparatus into one word, No. Borges branches his own forking paths outward into every possible future. This cycle branches inward, toward negation, each panel narrowing the space until nothing is left to branch.

**2. Ground with more than one kind of history under it**

Galveston earned a real reputation once as the Ellis Island of the West, processing more immigrants in the late nineteenth century than any American port besides New York and Baltimore, before it became the site of the deadliest natural disaster in the country's history. This cycle's fantasy of reinventing something between two people on that same shore is landing on ground that has already seen plenty of people do exactly that and never turn back.


### Galveston — I. Knees

**1. Ground with its own history of drowning**

Galveston is not a neutral name to invoke for a doomed trip. The 1900 hurricane that struck the island remains the deadliest natural disaster in recorded American history, and the citys own chief meteorologist had assured everybody in print, nine years earlier, that serious hurricane damage there was not worth speaking of. A fantasy of going to Galveston where the stars remember how to touch the water is landing, whether it knows it or not, on ground whose most famous historical fact is the water forgetting how to stay where it was supposed to.


### Galveston — II. Blue Hour

**1. A border that gets carried, not crossed**

Gloria Anzaldua argued the border is never simply a line you cross and leave behind, it is a condition you carry with you, undetermined, always in transition. Podria ser peor, mejor, quien sabe, spoken by strangers at a gas station this poem never actually reaches, carries a small piece of that same undetermined quality, a place approached in two languages at once and fully arrived at in neither.


### Galveston — III. Shotguns

**1. Wisdom literature already ran these numbers**

Proverbs weighed this exact question three thousand years ago, better a dinner of herbs where love is than a fattened ox and hatred with it, and came down firmly on the side of the herbs. Could we have held all that together, I wanted boring, I think you did too runs that identical arithmetic and still cannot quite commit to the answer Proverbs settled on without much apparent trouble.


### Galveston — IV. No

**1. The oldest one word verdict on wanting**

Ecclesiastes needed a repeated word to say no to a whole lifetime of striving, vanity of vanities, all is vanity. This cycle needs the word exactly once, to say no to a single imagined weekend. The scale is smaller. On the page, the finality is not.


### A Serranilla

**1. Santillanas form, with an ending he never needed**

The Marques de Santillana wrote the surviving classics of the serranilla in fifteenth century Castile, a nobleman riding the sierra undone by a peasant girl who answers him back rather than simply being looked at. This poem keeps his central encounter and adds something his own poems never needed, a death, the montero found beneath the evening star at the close. A courtly convention, given a murder ballads ending.


### No Serrana

**1. A title that argues with its own form**

No Serrana negates its own genre before the poem even starts, serrana, mountain girl, with no set flat in front of it. The poem then plays that negation out formally rather than just announcing it, the girl offered the old courtly frame and refusing to occupy it, once in English and once in her own Spanish, esa moza no quiere saber nada de amores. Form and content arguing in plain sight, which is about as clean a definition of irony as the New Critics ever wanted.


### In Another Garden

**1. The oldest English exile poem, standing behind this one**

The Wanderer, an Old English elegy from the Exeter Book, is spoken by a man who has lost his lord, his kin, and his homeland, and carries that loss with grim, disciplined restraint. This poem inverts the whole premise. England, what are you doing here is not a man exiled from home grieving its loss at a careful distance, it is home itself showing up uninvited, refusing to stay lost the way the old elegy assumes it will.


### Out Near Palo Duro

**1. Ground that already had a name before this poem got there**

Palo Duro Canyon is not neutral scenery. The Battle of Palo Duro Canyon in September 1874 effectively ended organized Comanche, Kiowa, and Cheyenne resistance across the Texas Panhandle, after federal forces destroyed a captured pony herd over a thousand animals strong and broke those bands ability to keep moving. A corrido set here, riding freely through that same ground, carries that history whether or not it ever says the words out loud.


### The Kid Meets Her in the Blue Lands

**1. A prophet arguing with an unfaithful people who still call him father**

The epigraph is Jeremiah 3:4, God addressing an Israel that has played the harlot with many lovers and still, out of old habit, calls him father. That is exactly the shape of the scene that follows, inconstancy answered by a claim of continued intimacy nobody involved has fully earned anymore. See Notes.md for the fuller argument this scene anchors, and the movements own note for why it sits at the dead center of the books reading order now, an ambush arrived at rather than a thesis announced up front.


### The Marques Dreams of Her

**1. Dido, wounded before the story even starts**

The Aeneid gives us Dido already lost in its opening lines of her own episode, the queen, long since wounded by grave care, love described as an injury before it is ever described as a feeling. A rusting arrow in the chest borrows that wound logic directly, and the poems own collision, Dido at Carthage, or a girl buying carnations at a market in California, repeats the move this book already made with Venus over Algiers Point, myth and errand list forced into the same sentence, neither one allowed to feel more real than the other.


### Another Girl at the Well of Time

**1. The oldest meet cute in scripture, and this poem breaks it**

Robert Alter names the recurring biblical scene, a man arrives at a well in a foreign land and meets the woman he will marry, Rebekah, Rachel, Zipporah, a type scene an ancient audience would recognize on sight the way we recognize a meet cute now. This poem stages the type scene faithfully, the rider, the foreign ground, the woman beside a trickling spring, and then breaks its expected outcome. She is already promised to Anton and sends him away. The form promises a marriage. The poem delivers a refusal instead, which is this whole movements catalogue of women in miniature.


## VI. Field Hymns and Hexes


### VI. Field Hymns and Hexes (section epigraph)

**1. A tree that gets no chase, only the aftermath**

Frazer's ghost runs this movement more than any other, the tree as god, the dying and reviving vegetation spirit. A Post Oak does not need Frazer to work, but it sits older than him too, Ovid's Daphne sealing over into laurel bark mid-flight to escape Apollo. This poem gives its post oak Daphne's ending with the chase subtracted, no man was made for it, motive removed and only the transformation left standing.

**2. Decoration or working magic, and the barns that never settled the question**

Pennsylvania Dutch hex signs, the barn stars the second half of this title distantly echoes, have never been fully sorted out by folklorists as protective magic or plain decoration, and probably cannot be sorted out from the barns alone. This movement keeps that same productive uncertainty running underneath its calm surface, naming a thing correctly in the right rhythm moving something in the world whether or not the namer understands what got moved.


### West of the Pine Line

**1. A mind that had to become nothing to see winter right**

Wallace Stevens argued you have to become nothing yourself before you can see a bare winter landscape correctly, no projected grief allowed to color an emptiness that is just, factually, empty. Even the dust lies still, stillness that lies ahead reaches for that same discipline, a boundary described without being mourned, trusted to be enough on its own without dressing it up in feeling that is not actually there.

**2. The moons own animal, gone still**

The hare or rabbit shows up as a lunar animal across an unusually wide spread of unrelated cultures, the Moon Rabbit in Chinese and Japanese folklore, a rabbit in the moon in Aztec tradition, the hare bound to old European fertility custom behind the modern Easter Bunny. This books moon obsession runs through movements I and IV both, which makes the jackrabbit still as stone here worth noticing as more than passing wildlife. It is the moons own animal, gone motionless, on a boundary even the wind avoids.


### For the Gila Trout

**1. A fish that carries more than its own color**

Jung traced the fish across centuries of iconography as a durable symbol of the psychic anima, something alive and elusive, visible only when it moves. A slip of fire flashing wet copper light is a fair image for exactly that kind of symbol, glimpsed and then gone the instant the water settles.

**2. Endangered before the country had the paperwork for it**

The Gila trout was placed on one of the countrys earliest endangered species lists back in 1966, seven years before the modern Endangered Species Act existed to replace that provisional law. The fish outlasted the very statute first written to save it.


### Knife Handles

**1. A blade that wards just by being present**

Cold iron is about as durable a piece of European folk magic as there is, iron and steel held across a wide range of otherwise unrelated traditions to turn back spirits and ill intent simply by being iron. A poem about knife handles carved from pine, a blade blue air drives down to crack the ground, is working in a register the old charm would recognize instantly. A blade does not have to swing to do its warding. Sometimes it only has to be there.

**2. The tool a man measures himself against**

Heaney measured his own pen against his fathers and grandfathers spades in Digging, deciding what he held could still count as the same kind of labor. This poem runs something close to that measurement in reverse, back in Arkansas, where rain fell in sheets set directly against pines recast here as the handle of a blade. Heaney chose his instrument. This poem is still deciding what its hands are actually holding.


### Black River Love (cycle)

**1. Three witnesses, moralizing inward instead of outward**

A finch, a transformed woman, a garfish, stand in for what the medieval Physiologus would have called a bestiary sequence, except the Physiologus moralized outward, a pelican becoming an allegory of Christ, where this cycle moralizes inward, each creature disclosing nothing about the world, only about the man watching it watch him back. The three move from tenderness to metamorphosis to something that predates tenderness entirely, love treated here less like an event than a geological layer.

**2. Real water, not an invented composite**

The Black River is an actual tributary of the White River, running through the same Arkansas Ozark country this book keeps returning to under different titles. Three witnesses to love get set beside a real, mappable body of water, which grounds the cycle's mythology in a place a reader could go stand beside, whether or not anything mythological happens to be occurring there that day.


### Black River Love — I. To A Pine's Finch

**1. Attention paid without needing a metaphor to carry it**

John Clare worked the land he wrote about and paid it the same close, unsentimental attention regardless of whether the poem needed elevating. His reputation as a serious poet, not a rural curiosity, took the better part of a century to be taken fully seriously. I saw you, and everything kept growing is Clares whole practice in five words, attention paid at close range with nothing standing in to do the noticing for it.


### Black River Love — II. A Post Oak

**1. Frost wanted a boy. This poem does not ask for one.**

Frost preferred to imagine a boy swinging his birches bent rather than admit ice storms had simply bent them, the human story being kinder than the weather. This poem offers no such kinder alternative. I saw it drag its roots through stone, I saw it live and die alone states the transformation without any effort to soften it. Frost wanted permission to believe a story. This poem does not ask for any.

**2. A litany, not a metaphor**

I saw it once repeats and varies for eighteen lines, closer to Ecclesiastes own litany, I have seen all the works, than to a single controlling image. Litany does not argue. It accumulates until the accumulation itself becomes the argument, which is exactly how a woman becomes a tree here, not by one transformation but by enough repeated seeing that the seeing does the transforming.


### Black River Love — III. Garfish

**1. Older than it has any right to still be**

A fish thought extinct alongside the dinosaurs turned up alive in a fishermans net off South Africa in December 1938, the coelacanth, a lineage that never got the message it was supposed to have already stopped existing. Grimed with hunger older than rainfall, older than loving is not much of an exaggeration next to that fact. This garfishs age is closer to plain biology than to poetic license, and like the coelacanth, it was never particularly impressed by the time it was supposed to have not survived.


### Palo Santo

**1. Smoke that is supposed to be going somewhere**

Let my prayer be set forth before thee as incense, the Psalm says, and rising smoke as a vehicle between the human and the divine is close to a universal ritual technology, Catholic Mass, Vedic fire offerings, palo santo and copal alike. It holds the stillness open like a tomb keeps that usual upward motion strangely suspended here, neither rising to deliver anything nor put out, held the way the whole poem is held, mid transit.

**2. A companion ritual two movements over**

This poem is worth reading beside Instructions for Smelling Vetiver in movement VIII, a structural pair, two poems built as scent-ritual instructions for reaching a woman who is not present, one incense and one plant, the same longing translated into two different pharmacopeias.


### She Circles the Lake Without Asking

**1. Dantes wolf, or something closer to a guide**

Dantes Inferno opens on a lupa, a she-wolf, usually read as appetite that will not stop circling and will not be satisfied. This she-wolf circles too, but the poem insists twice that circling is not hunger, not in hunt, not in waiting, not in trance. Closer, maybe, to the wolf-kin that circumpolar and North Asian shamanic tradition casts as psychopomps, guides at ease on ground human travelers experience as threshold. A guide does not need her circling explained to be trusted at it.


### The Kid Dreams of Early Summer

**1. Weston with a fake mustache on**

The epigraphs Gospel of Jessie James is not a joke about the outlaw. It is Jessie L. Weston, the scholar whose From Ritual to Romance supplied Eliot the entire vegetation-myth apparatus behind The Waste Land, wearing a borrowed name. The ecstatic vision that follows earns the weight of that borrowing, let us eat and drink of what we could not pluck alone sits close enough to Song of Solomon, eat, o friends, drink, yea, drink abundantly, o beloved, that the echo reads as deliberate, feast language borrowed from the one book in the whole tradition unembarrassed about desire being holy rather than merely dangerous.

**2. A vision this total needs some account for itself**

THE WHOLE ROOM FLASHED WHITE reaches toward the Eleusinian Mysteries, whose culminating vision was so heavily guarded by oath that almost nothing certain survives about what initiates actually saw, and toward Saint John of the Crosss brighter companion to his Dark Night, the Spiritual Canticle, where union arrives through a wine cellar and a meadow rather than through darkness. Whatever this poem saw, it does not try to explain it either. It just capitalizes.


## VII. It Speaks In Judgment


### VII. It Speaks In Judgment (section epigraph)

**1. Justice withdrawn from the sky, judgment moved to a rock instead**

Virgil's Justice quitting the earth is the myth of Astraea, last of the immortals to leave a corrupted mankind, fixed afterward in the sky as Virgo. The epigraph is already about judgment withdrawing from the world, which makes Six White Horses an answer to it, judgment has not withdrawn here, it just changed address, from a goddess in the sky to a jaguar bound up with Tezcatlipoca in Aztec cosmology, lord of memory and retribution, whose obsidian mirror shows a man his true face whether he wants it or not.

**2. A weighing, not a verdict**

Six horses, six small interrogations embedded in the killing like a catechism, sits closer to the weighing of the heart before Anubis than to any single Western judgment scene, not one verdict, but the heart tested piece by piece. The Malleus Maleficarum turned judgment into a fixed, repeatable script, question after question, regardless of what evidence stood in front of the judge. This jaguar interrogates by that same method, though the poem grants him something the Malleus never granted anyone it examined, an apparent willingness to actually listen to the answer.


### Six White Horses

**1. A judge with real teeth in Mesoamerican religion**

The jaguar is not an invented syncretism here. He belongs to Tezcatlipoca in Aztec cosmology, lord of the night sky, memory, and retribution, whose obsidian mirror shows a man his own true face whether he wants to see it or not, and the flat rock he waits on echoes the techcatl, the real sacrificial stone shaped to arch a victims chest for the blade, plain looking until it is already being used. I dont eat men, I remember them is Tezcatlipocas whole job description handed to an animal.

**2. Six trials, not six killings**

Six horses, six short interrogations before each death, do you even remember her voice, no, only how it left, has more in common with the Tibetan Bardo Thodol than with a simple string of killings. The Bardo describes a sequence of visionary states the dying consciousness passes through, each one a test of whether it can recognize what it is seeing without being seized by fear of it. The jaguar is not delivering these horses to rest. He is testing, one at a time, whether each can meet its own reckoning honestly, and more than one plainly cannot.


## VIII. Laws, Money, Women, Land


### VIII. Laws, Money, Women, Land (section epigraph)

**1. Four things that never come to anybody here in surplus**

Virgil's greedy farmer, harvest bursting the barns twice over, sits ironic over a movement whose own title names the four things nobody in it ever gets a surplus of, laws, money, women, land, the same sequence Deuteronomy uses to organize a whole society's obligations, property, marriage, land, laid out in order. Deuteronomy presents that order as covenant. This movement presents it as inheritance nobody asked for and nobody gets to renegotiate.

**2. A trickster who never asks, only beckons**

Hoodoo and conjure practice, real and documented, runs under There Are Bones In Her Mouth the way it runs under a good deal of the actual rural South, Hurston's Mules and Men is still the plainest firsthand account of it. Sonnet(ish) runs its own devil figure, the Piper, through a lineage that splits and never quite chooses, Browning's debt collector and the blues crossroads myth both, going back further still to Papa Legba, the trickster guardian of the crossroads in African diasporic legend. He never asked. He only beckoned is Legba's whole job description, more than it is Browning's.


### Mayday

**1. Two mayday distresses layered on top of each other**

The radio distress call comes from the French m'aidez, help me. Sea witches from Devon to the Orkneys sold sailors knotted ropes, one knot untied for a breeze, two for strong wind, three for a gale, a documented practice that shows up in an actual 1617 Vardo witch trial confession. This braid's a binding thread, it loops the vows that once I told, one knot for each sweet word I said redirects that same three-knot logic from weather to courtship, released word by word instead of gale by gale.


### Instructions for Smelling Vetiver

**1. A Georgic in numbered instructions**

Virgils own poems are didactic instruction verse dressed as epic, how to farm turned into song. This poems numbered steps, First, open the screen door, Second, let the thunder peel your name, keep that same didactic form and empty out the farming, using it instead to walk a man through grief with a plant as the only permitted vocabulary. Vetiver itself carries real ritual use beyond perfumery, burned for protection in Haitian Vodou practice, woven as khus in South Asian tradition to cool and purify a room. The instructions treat it as a rite completed, not a scent merely noticed.


### Breeze. Palm.

**1. Two sacred texts where a palm answers a woman in distress**

The righteous shall flourish like the palm tree, the Psalm says, and the tree carries triumphant arrival straight through to Palm Sunday. The Quran gives Mary childbirth alone beneath a palm that is commanded and answers, dropping ripe dates exactly when she needs them. This poems own palm gives no such answer, swaying to no gospel, no angel arc, addressed at length and questioned without once shaking anything loose. Maryams palm answers. This one is only asked.

**2. Orestes under a Gulf Coast palm**

Orestes wept beneath one, in the shade of his sisters voice reaches past the wind-god Aeolus into Attic tragedy directly, Orestes and Electra bound by a fathers murder neither can walk away from. Grafting that old family grief onto a swaying Gulf Coast palm, a long-legged metronome shaking off pieces of sky, is the poems whole method, borrowing the weight of the oldest family tragedy on record and setting it down in a parking lot.


### There Are Bones In Her Mouth (cycle)

**1. Three technologies carrying the same ghost**

Three panels move the identical folk material through three different ways of keeping a memory alive. A Prophecy is spoken, pure Hurston-territory conjure speech. Catahoula Leopard Bitch is embodied, the working familiar made flesh and fur. Dead Meme, Mississippi is digital, its refrain, you laugh, you scroll, you forget, broken and reassembled like a losing signal, a formula in the exact sense Milman Parry and Albert Lord gave the word studying how Homer got remembered, swift-footed Achilles, wine-dark sea, units built to survive minds that cannot hold the whole of a thing at once.

**2. Why a tooth, and not some other bone**

Teeth outlast the mouths they came from, which is why folk tradition keeps reaching for them specifically when it needs something durable enough to carry a person's identity past their own decay. There Are Bones In Her Mouth names the whole cycle's method in its title, permanence pulled out of a body and kept working long after the rest of that body has stopped being available.


### There Are Bones In Her Mouth — I. A Prophecy

**1. What the cycles own title is describing**

Throwing the bones, a real divinatory practice across African and African diasporic tradition, casting small bones or shells and reading the pattern they fall into, is what this whole cycles title is naming. Momma Magdalene never touches an actual bone, only a radio playing static and barometric pressure, droning like the Scripture, but the title has already told the reader what kind of reading is underway before she says a word. Her own name carries its own folk conflation too, a prophetess named for the Magdalene long and wrongly merged with an unnamed penitent sinner in Luke, a borrowed and slightly wrong authority handed to a woman doing wrong-authority work of her own.


### There Are Bones In Her Mouth — II. Catahoula Leopard Bitch

**1. Kin to the black dogs of the old country**

The Catahoula Leopard Dog is real, Louisianas own state dog, bred for hog hunting in the swamps that gave the breed its name. She keeps company with a wider folklore of spectral hounds, Black Shuck along the East Anglian coast, the Barghest in Yorkshire, the Cwn Annwn of Wales who hunt souls rather than haunt houses. English witch trial testimony insisted a familiar and its keeper were bound by something a court could point to, a mark, a suckling. This dog gets no such tidy bond. She comes and goes on her own schedule, belonging to no one the poem is willing to call a witch outright.


### There Are Bones In Her Mouth — III. Dead Meme, Mississippi

**1. A formula built to survive being forgotten**

You laugh, you scroll, you forget, broken and reassembled across the poem like a signal losing bars, is a formula in the exact technical sense Milman Parry and Albert Lord gave the word studying how Homer got composed and remembered, swift-footed Achilles, wine-dark sea, repeated units built to survive minds that cannot hold the whole of a thing at once. A meme is that same technology dressed for a worse century.

**2. A burial that mocks the rite instead of completing it**

Sophocles built Antigone on the terror of a body left unburied, exposed, treated as a genuine cosmic wrong. This corpse gets a burial of sorts, wrapped in a Garfield blanket, but it plays closer to the medieval Danse Macabre, which was often genuinely comic, skeletal Death mocking rank and dignity alike, than to Antigones grief. The line between desecration and comedy turns out thinner than either tradition wanted to admit.


### Sonnet(ish) (cycle)

**1. A form loosened on purpose, not out of laziness**

Sonnet is doing real formal work, not hedging. The sonnet sequence has a long habit of chronicling one love across many poems in a fixed, disciplined form, Petrarch's Canzoniere, Shakespeare's own hundred fifty four. This cycle keeps the chronicling impulse, five poems tracking one Piper across a rough narrative, while loosening the form itself, because the thing being chronicled will not hold still either. The Aesthetic of the Wound is where that loosened form does its clearest work, Donne's trick of yoking violently unlike things together turned against devotion instead of in service of it.

**2. The chain this cycle deliberately refuses**

A corona of sonnets locks its poems together so tight none of them can be read alone, the last line of each becoming the first line of the next. This cycle shares a subject across five poems, the Piper, the Lord, but refuses that chain entirely, which matches its own title's hedge exactly, close enough to the disciplined form to invoke it, unwilling to actually submit to the discipline.


### Sonnet(ish) — I. A Piper's Warning

**1. A debt collector wearing whatever name the story needs**

Hamelin town records reference a real loss of children in 1284 with no agreed explanation, plague, a Childrens Crusade, or a recruited emigration east, the Piper himself maybe a garbled memory of a real recruiter. Blues scholarship has argued the crossroads bargain legend actually belonged first to Tommy Johnson, not the better remembered Robert Johnson, before drifting to whichever man got more famous. The myth keeps outgrowing whoever it started with. He never asked. He only beckoned works from that same drift, an identity that matters less than the fact somebody, historically, always turns out to have followed.


### Sonnet(ish) — II. The Aesthetic of the Wound

**1. Instruments of suffering, improvised from the house**

Medieval devotion kept a genre for exactly this, the Arma Christi, the nails and crown and cords of the Passion depicted individually as objects for contemplation. A piano string, a braid of copper ants is an Arma Christi for a century with no real nails left to venerate, improvising its own instruments out of whatever is lying around. Bataille argued eroticism, death, and the sacred all share one root in the human need to approach a limit. Dont lie, you love Him most when He wont die is close to Batailles whole argument folded into a couplet, worship and desire and death refused the courtesy of being treated as three separate things.


### Sonnet(ish) — III. He Danced Me To Dust

**1. Dancing as the affliction, and dancing as the cure**

Strasbourg, 1518, is real documented history, dozens to hundreds of people dancing uncontrollably for days, some reportedly to death, blamed at the time on poisoning, hysteria, or a fiddling devil. Southern Italy kept the opposite belief, tarantism, where a tarantulas bite required the same frenzied dance, the tarantella, as its only cure, the dancing understood as the poison working its way back out. This poem never says which kind of dancing this is. He whistled by the henhouse light, stalked by night in tall wet grass leaves both possibilities open, exactly the question neither tradition ever fully settled either.


### Sonnet(ish) — IV. The Lord with a New Face

**1. A body the early church spent centuries insisting was not an illusion**

Docetism held that Christs body was never fully real, only seeming to suffer, a heresy orthodoxy fought hard against for exactly that reason. Matthew gives the counter-doctrine, inasmuch as ye have done it unto the least of these, ye have done it unto me, Christ hidden in whoever is least likely to be recognized as him. He walks out again, in your disguise takes that doctrine to its most uncomfortable place, not Christ hidden in the deserving poor, but hidden in whatever a readers own hungers happen to be wearing that week.


### Sonnet(ish) — V. In a Far Country

**1. A departure with no homecoming waiting on the other end**

The title is Luke 15:13 almost exactly, the Prodigal Son who took his journey into a far country, and any reader who knows the parable spends the whole poem waiting on the return that never comes. I will build a chapel out of juniper and ash is this speakers own ending, self-built rather than granted by a waiting father. I burned my letters in the grease of the stove belongs to a real and widespread folk practice besides, burning correspondence to sever a bond on the theory that what the fire takes, the connection goes with it, the positive-magic mirror of the curse tablets already at work back in movement II.


## IX. Doors and Small Devotions


### IX. Doors and Small Devotions (section epigraph)

**1. Rooms and an inn, doing work no ordinary room or inn could do**

Teresa of Avila mapped the soul as a castle of many rooms nested inward toward God, entered one door at a time. Fragment 1's dark inn, servant of penny paid pilgrims sits close enough to John of the Cross's dark night that I doubt the resemblance is accidental. Both mystics used ordinary domestic architecture, rooms, doors, an inn, to describe something with nothing domestic in it at all. This movement does the same, lower ceilings, worse plumbing.

**2. Small, on purpose, like a novena instead of a lifetime vigil**

A novena scales devotion down to nine days, something a person can actually sustain rather than a lifetime of watching. This movement's own small devotions work that same scale, modest, repeatable poems instead of the grand classical apparatus the earlier movements borrow. The modesty is not an accident. By this point in the book, it has stopped believing grand apparatus is what a person actually needs to get through a Tuesday.


### Begin With A Door

**1. A man who waited his whole life outside the one door that was his**

Kafkas parable Before the Law has a man wait his entire life outside a door built specifically for him, deference mistaken for the right approach until it is too late for any approach at all. This door is the same door, the kingdom on the other side has always been this kingdom reverses that whole premise gently, a door tried, closed, and returned to rather than only waited outside of. Kafkas warning is about permanent hesitation. This poem at least keeps walking back up to knock.

**2. A god who looks both ways from the same threshold**

Rome kept a god just for doorways, Janus, two faces looking opposite directions at once, a month still carrying his name. That is a fully Janus shaped claim this poem is making, not two different rooms on either side of a door, but one god looking both ways from the same threshold without ever needing to pick a direction.


### Standing There, Still Real

**1. The walking was never separate from what it was walking toward**

Korzybski warned the map is not the territory. Merleau-Ponty went further, arguing the body is not a vehicle carrying a mind through a separately perceived world, it is the very site where perceiving and being happen as one act. The map turned out to be the walking is closer to Merleau-Pontys claim than to a simple warning ignored, not the map is wrong, but there was never a map standing apart from the walking to begin with.


### Fragment 1

**1. Vessels broken by too much light, not too little**

Shackles! and this poems falling, fractured syntax anticipate a concept the Codas own epigraph will name directly, shevirat ha-kelim, the Lurianic idea of vessels built to hold divine light that shattered under its own intensity, scattering sparks now trapped in broken shells. Lurias system offers tikkun, repair, gathering those sparks home. This poem does not reach that far. It stops at the shattering, mid-syllable, the same place George Herberts shaped poems and, further along, e e cummings broken word l(a already knew language sometimes has to fracture on the page before it can carry what it is holding.


### The Last Real Girl (cycle)

**1. Waters wept beside, twice over**

By the waters of Salton we sat and wept answers Psalm 137 almost word for word, though Babylon at least remembers a Zion existed to grieve. The institutional announcement collage running through all three poems, PLEASE HOLD YOUR BREATH, NO FUTURE REFUNDS, belongs to the same appropriative tradition as Kenneth Goldsmith's uncreative writing, found civic language relocated into verse on the theory that it already carries more despair unedited than any poet could add to it.

**2. Final, the way a horror film means it**

Carol Clover coined final girl for the one character, almost always a woman, who survives a slasher film by outlasting everyone else on screen. This cycle borrows that shape of lastness without the violence, the last real girl surviving not a killer but every earlier claim on the word real that the cycle itself has already run through and used up. See Notes.md for how this cycle answers Standing There, Still Real, the sincere claim right next to its own collapse into billboard copy.


### The Last Real Girl — East Again, West Again

**1. A direction with death already built into it**

Shelley asked the west wind to be both destroyer and preserver at once. Egyptian religion put the land of the dead in the west, following the sun down, going west surviving in English as an honest to God euphemism for dying. Cavafy warned a man convinced that leaving his city would finally let him become somebody else that the city would follow him regardless, no new lands, no other seas. West. West. Ill say it again, West is spoken with the exact desperation of someone who has not yet had Cavafys warning, or has had it and is refusing, this once, to believe it applies to him.


### The Last Real Girl — Please Hold Your Breath

**1. Holding your breath was never just about air**

By the waters of Salton we sat and wept answers Psalm 137 almost word for word, by the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down and wept, except Babylon at least remembers a Zion existed to grieve. Hebrew ruach, Greek pneuma, Latin spiritus, every one of those languages uses a single word for both breath and soul. PLEASE HOLD YOUR BREATH, UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE, lifted from an airport announcement, is doing more theological work than its bureaucratic register lets on, an instruction to hold the soul in place and wait, dressed up as gate policy.


### The Last Real Girl — Seeing the Real Girl At Last

**1. Advertising copy, stolen back and turned on itself**

NO FUTURE REFUNDS and THE SUN HAS BEEN REPLACED BY A MORE AFFORDABLE SUN read like real billboard copy because they are built the same way the Situationists built detournement, hijacking existing advertising language and turning it back against the culture that would have produced it sincerely. Debord called this the society of the spectacle. Baudrillard pushed further still, arguing the fake sun does not merely hide the real one, it replaces it so completely the distinction stops mattering. This poem does not need a poets touch to be devastating. It only needed noticing and leaving alone.


### He Finds the Pain of Loss

**1. The wall between them is older than this poem**

Passing notes through a wall with a gap too narrow for anything but paper is Pyramus and Thisbes wall exactly, Ovids lovers who speak through a crack because their families forbid anything closer, a crack that eventually gets them killed by misunderstanding across it. This poem does not go that far, but this botched and beautiful attempt is the same crack, centuries on, still the only aperture two people too walled off to touch can manage, and still, somehow, love.


## Coda


### Coda (section epigraph)

**1. A tail, doing exactly what a tail is supposed to do**

Coda, Italian for tail, closes a piece formally, sometimes by bringing back a theme heard earlier rather than introducing anything new. This movement does exactly that, correct to the term it borrows, seven worlds answering Harmonikum's seven voices clear back at the start. If the book has a homecoming in it, a nostos, this is where it would happen, and it is worth being honest that it does not, quite.

**2. Restated, not resolved, and that was always the plan**

If the mirror was cracked or just remembering you differently is the whole book's paradox said one more time, not answered, because solving it was never the assignment. Frye would call landing in neither comedy's return nor tragedy's finished fall a refusal of both at once. I would agree with him, and add that the refusal is not a failure of nerve. It is the only honest ending a book this committed to an unresolved paradox was ever going to be able to give you.


### The Kid Along The Frontage Road

**1. A hallucinated companion, borrowed honestly**

Who is the third who always walks beside you is Eliot lifted almost whole from The Waste Land, and Eliots own note there cites Shackletons Antarctic expedition, men so worn down by cold and hunger they became convinced one more man was walking with them than could actually be counted. Same delusion, moved from an ice shelf to a frontage road. A man alone long enough starts counting extra footsteps regardless of the ground under him.

**2. A vessel broken by too much light, and no repair promised**

The inwards of a fool are like a broken vessel sits right on top of shevirat ha-kelim, the Lurianic idea of vessels shattered under the weight of the light they were built to hold, sparks scattered into broken shells still waiting on tikkun, repair. I was not born to end anything, then crossed it out is a man circling that vocabulary, ending, mending, finishing, and backing off each word before it can commit him to having done it.

**3. An ending that turns back to touch its own beginning**

Eliot closed East Coker by reversing its own opening line, in my beginning is my end becoming in my end is my beginning, refusing to just repeat itself the easy way. This book tries something at that same scale, seven worlds here answering seven voices clear back in Harmonikum. I will not claim it lands with Eliots precision. But an ending that turns back to touch where it started is not a failure to move forward. For a book this committed to a paradox it never intends to solve, it is about the only honest kind of ending there was ever going to be.
