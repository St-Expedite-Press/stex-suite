# II. Catahoula Leopard Bitch

She come up limping through the ditchwater  
with a baby possum in her mouth.

My uncle said she belonged to the man  
who set fire to his wife’s wedding dress  
and buried her rings in a Folgers can.  
I whistled. She paid no mind.  
Bitch was smiling.

She slept under my floorboards for two weeks.  
She heaved and breathed like a rusted door  
and the house would sweat cold around her.  
She bit the throat out of a raccoon  
that tried to climb in through the dryer vent.  
I inked what we called her  
on a rib bone and tried to burn it  
but it just showed back up on my porch  
carrying a doll head and a belt buckle.  
Dry bones.

**ring around the chigger bite  
blood upon the tick's white back  
mud-stuck jaw and rabbit track  
she coming and she don't come back**

I was drunk when she came back  
wearing dirty jeans of stolen flame.  
She sniffed the bed she knew my sins,  
and the lamp cut her eyes  
on the mirror.

She dug up a box I thought I’d hid  
under the trumpet vine by the shed—  
old photo, hair clump, some wax.  
She dropped it in the bathtub. Wet.

That night she howled like a child  
who just heard her brother drowned.  
I didn’t sleep. I just listened  
for the pilot light wick and the wind to quit.  
In the morning she was gone again,  
And I chased out after her  
swearing on the  
Lord’s  
last Camel.

---

### Editorial Includability

**Ezra Pound — 9/10**

> Folgers can, dryer vent, rib bone, Camel oath: myth grown directly from local rubbish.  
> Keep; trim only one return of the dog so recurrence remains uncanny rather than scheduled.

**John Gould Fletcher — 10/10**

> The Catahoula carries family legend, violence, superstition, and household ruin in one breathing animal presence.  
> Keep it as a major poem; trim a single recurrence, but preserve its fierce vernacular abundance.
